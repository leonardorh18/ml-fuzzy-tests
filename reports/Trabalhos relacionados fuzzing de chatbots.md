## Grammar coverage, not code coverage, is the untested step

Grammar-based generation goes back to Purdom's 1972 sentence generator. The modern line runs from LangFuzz through Nautilus and Gramatron, and it always targets formal languages (JavaScript, C, XML, JSON) that have a parser and crash oracles. The step closest to the paper is the expansion-coverage strategy in *The Fuzzing Book*, which **Havrikov & Zeller formalized as k-path coverage**. Their result that input-structure coverage correlates with and predicts code coverage is what justifies using grammar coverage as an adequacy proxy when code is invisible, as it is for hosted chatbots ([Havrikov & Zeller 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ase.2019.00027)).

**Gap.** Every grammar fuzzer above derives *program-like* inputs and judges them with crash or sanitizer oracles. None derives natural-language prompts, and none uses grammar coverage as the only feedback available against a black-box generative model. Our work carries the GrammarCoverageFuzzer strategy (1-path coverage) to a prompt grammar whose derivations also carry their own ground truth, which is the "verifiable prompt" idea. The nearest NL-side neighbors are templates (CheckList), fixed three-part compositions (FuzzLLM) and a transformation DSL (h4rm3l). None of them is a recursive CFG with coverage feedback.

## Differential testing always assumed a shared specification

Differential testing started with McKeeman's idea of running one generated program through several compilers. Csmith made it the standard method by generating only valid C programs, so that every disagreement means something ([Yang et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1993498.1993532)). DeepXplore carried it to ML by treating disagreement among several DNNs as an error signal without labels ([Pei et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3132747.3132785)). The key departure for the paper is that **chat LLMs do not share a formal specification**, so a disagreement is only a *signal*. Verifiable ground truth decides which model is wrong, and it also catches "consensus-wrong" cases that a pure differential oracle cannot see. SAC3 argues this second point directly: models can be confidently and consistently wrong ([Zhang et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2023.findings-emnlp.1032)).

**Gap.** Classic differential testing assumes that every implementation follows one specification, so any divergence proves a bug. The ML variants compare classifiers or library backends. LLM-related differential work almost always uses the LLM as a *test generator* for other software (Fuzz4All, Differential Prompting, Mokav), and hallucination work (SAC3, SelfCheckGPT) is not framed as testing. Our work applies a Csmith-style valid-input generator and a DeepXplore-style cross-model oracle to chat LLMs, and it resolves blame with grammar-carried ground truth. That last step turns a disagreement signal into a verdict and exposes consensus failures.

## Metamorphic oracles for LLMs stay inside one model

The test-oracle problem is the canonical framing for testing chatbots. In Barr et al.'s taxonomy, our design pairs a **derived/metamorphic oracle** (cross-paraphrase invariance) with **specified oracles** from verifiable tasks ([Barr et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.2014.2372785)). The case for paraphrase-based testing is quantitative. Single-prompt evaluation is brittle across **6.5M instances, 20 LLMs and 39 tasks** ([Mizrahi et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00681)). Pretrained LMs show "poor" consistency under meaning-preserving rephrasing ([Elazar et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00410)). The largest LLM metamorphic study collected **191 MRs and ran about 560K tests** ([Cho et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSME64153.2025.00025)).

**Gap.** Metamorphic LLM testing (METAL, LLMorph, MORTAR) checks each model against itself, and an MR violation shows inconsistency without saying which answer is wrong. Drowzee and ReCode add ground truth, but they do not generate inputs from a coverage-guided grammar or compare models. Our work lets the grammar generate the paraphrase family *and* the ground truth together. That gives three oracles from one derivation: cross-paraphrase invariance, cross-model agreement, and correctness. One distinction should be stated explicitly: self-consistency (Wang et al., ICLR 2023) votes over *output samples* to improve accuracy, whereas we test invariance over *input paraphrases* to find failures.

## LLM fuzzing hunts jailbreaks, not everyday task failures

Existing LLM fuzzers use an attack-success oracle. GPTFuzzer mutates jailbreak templates AFL-style ([Yu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10253)), FuzzLLM composes template, constraint and question parts ([Yao et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp48485.2024.10448041)), and h4rm3l synthesizes attacks in a DSL. Meanwhile the NLP literature has documented exactly the functional failures the paper targets. Format changes alone swing LLaMA-2-13B accuracy by **up to 76 points** ([Sclar et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11324)). Open models lag closed ones in format adherence ([Xia et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18667)). Models "know the spelling of their tokens, yet fail to use this information" to manipulate text ([Edman et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-main.177)).

**Gap.** LLM fuzzing targets security (jailbreak or injection success), and chatbot testing in SE targets task-based, Dialogflow-style bots (MutaBot, Charm, Clerissi et al. 2026). Tokenization, counting and format failures are documented through *static benchmarks* on mostly large models. Our work turns these known weaknesses into **automatically generated, coverage-guided, self-checking test inputs** and runs them differentially across small open chat models from Hugging Face. That turns benchmark observations into a reproducible fuzzing procedure that also logs runtime/API errors as their own failure class.

## Conclusion

The literature lets the paper make a precise positioning claim instead of a vague novelty claim. It is the GrammarCoverageFuzzer strategy (1-path coverage), carried from formal languages to natural-language prompts, judged by a Csmith/DeepXplore-style cross-model oracle and a CheckList-style invariance oracle, with blame settled by ground truth that the grammar derivation itself carries. Each clause has a canonical citation, and their combination has none, so the Related Work section can show the gap clause by clause. A second, less obvious contribution falls out of this: hallucination-detection work (SAC3, SelfCheckGPT) and SE testing work (DeepXplore, METAL) both use disagreement as a signal but almost never cite each other, and connecting them under "differential oracle" is itself useful framing.

Two practical cautions follow. Because a cross-model oracle cannot see shared errors (SAC3's warning), the evaluation should report consensus-wrong cases separately, and that is only possible because the prompts are verifiable. Before submission, resolve the flagged metadata: the McKeeman 1998 primary page, the TOSEM k-path DOI, the h4rm3l author list, the Fuzzing Book BibTeX and the PromptRobust year. Then rerun one arXiv/DBLP search to confirm that no 2025–2026 preprint already pairs grammar-coverage prompt generation with cross-model differential testing.
