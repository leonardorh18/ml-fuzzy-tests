Chat models distributed through the Hugging Face Hub are increasingly embedded in software, yet they are hard to test: their input is natural language, they rarely crash, and most prompts have no single correct answer. We investigate whether grammar-based fuzzing combined with differential testing across models and metamorphic testing across paraphrases is an effective way to find functional failures in such models, and where its oracles fail. Our tool, \code{chatfuzz}, derives prompts from a context-free grammar whose derivations carry their own expected answer (arithmetic, letter counting, string reversal, JSON output, verbatim echo of hard Unicode text) and re-derives each test as paraphrases. We ran 170 prompts on three small open chat models (Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, SmolLM2-360M-Instruct, and TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat) with greedy decoding. Of 420 graded responses, 271 (64.5\%) failed their ground-truth oracle; pass rates ranged from 19\% to 47\%, string reversal was solved once in 90 attempts, and in 25 of 150 model--skeleton pairs a model answered some paraphrases correctly and others incorrectly. Without ground truth, the differential oracle flagged 98.8\% of the failures with 73.9\% precision but could not say which model was wrong; the metamorphic oracle was more precise (88.0\%) and less complete (79.5\%). Only one prompt received the same wrong answer from all three models. Coverage-guided expansion reached full grammar coverage in 27 of 30 seeds against 4 of 30 for random expansion, but only slightly increased the diversity of revealed failures. A manual check of 60 responses agreed with the automatic verdicts in 95\% of cases, and every disagreement came from the strictness of the JSON oracle. The results suggest that verifiable grammar derivations make differential and metamorphic signals actionable, while oracle design, rather than input generation, limits what can be concluded.
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} software testing; grammar-based fuzzing; differential testing; metamorphic testing; large language models.
