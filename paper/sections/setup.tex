\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Systems under test.} We selected three small instruction-tuned chat models from different families, so that differences between them are not artifacts of a shared training pipeline (Table~\ref{tab:models}). All three are distributed through the Hugging Face Hub and ran locally, with revisions pinned, greedy decoding, and at most 256 new tokens. We implemented the client for the hosted Hugging Face Inference Providers API but did not use it in the experiments, because no API token was available in the execution environment; the systems under test are therefore the models as served by \code{transformers}, not a hosted API.

\textbf{Environment.} A Linux cloud container with 4 CPU cores and 15\,GB of RAM, no GPU, Python 3.13, PyTorch 2.14.1, and \code{transformers} 5.21.0. Latencies are wall-clock times per reply on this machine, with one request at a time.

\textbf{Main campaign (RQ1, RQ2, RQ4).} With grammar seed 0 and $b = 0.8$, the generator produced 60 skeletons, 10 per category, each derived as up to 3 paraphrases. The \code{echo} templates admit only two distinct wordings, so the campaign has 170 prompts (161 distinct strings, because some skeletons repeat) and 510 replies. The 140 prompts of the five verifiable categories yield 420 graded replies; the 90 \code{edge} replies are inspected manually. Every model received the same prompts.

\textbf{Ablation 1: grammar coverage without models (RQ3).} For 30 grammar seeds, we generated 120 prompts with uniform random expansion ($b = 0$) and with coverage-guided expansion ($b = 0.8$), and recorded grammar coverage after each prompt.

\textbf{Ablation 2: failure diversity with models (RQ3).} For grammar seeds 0, 1, and 2 and both values of $b$, we ran 30 skeletons with one wording each on the two faster models, Qwen2.5 and SmolLM2 (60 replies per condition, 360 in total). We compare the number of failures, the number of distinct grammar expansions that appear in failing prompts (\emph{revealing rules}), and the number of distinct failure signatures (model, category, oracle verdict, and top-level template).

\textbf{Oracle validation (RQ4).} We drew a stratified random sample of four graded replies per model and verifiable category (60 replies, sampling seed 0). For each reply we recorded two judgments: whether the reply gives the correct answer, tolerating extra text that does not change it (\emph{content}), and whether it also follows the requested format, e.g.\ ``only the number'' (\emph{strict}). We compare both with the automatic verdict. In addition, we labeled all 90 \code{edge} replies as benign, refusal, hallucinated or wrong, degenerate repetition, or chat-template leakage. The judgments were made with the assistance of an AI model and are pending independent review by the authors (see the AI use disclosure).

\textbf{Analysis.} We report proportions with 95\% Wilson score intervals and compare models on the same prompts with the exact McNemar test. For RQ2 we restrict the analysis to \code{arith}, \code{count}, \code{reverse}, and \code{json}, whose canonical answers are independent of the oracle verdict (360 replies). A reply is \emph{flagged by the differential oracle} if its canonical answer, or the absence of one, differs from that of at least one other model on the same prompt, and \emph{flagged by the metamorphic oracle} if the model did not give the same canonical answer to all paraphrases of the skeleton. Recall is the share of ground-truth failures that are flagged; precision is the share of flagged replies that are failures. All statistics are computed by \code{experiments/paper\_stats.py} from the saved replies.
