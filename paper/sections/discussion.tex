\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Input generation was not the bottleneck; oracles were.} Every verifiable category revealed failures in every model, and coverage guidance made the grammar exercise all of its expansions with fewer prompts. Finding failures in models of this size is easy. What the campaign can conclude, however, depends on decisions made in the oracles: whether a JSON object inside prose counts (0\% or 83\% for TinyLlama), whether the last integer of a reply is its answer, whether a copied payload followed by a repetition loop is a copy. Our oracles are lenient in some categories and strict in another, and RQ4 shows that this asymmetry, not random noise, accounts for their disagreement with manual judgment. A test campaign on chat models should therefore report the oracle's definition of correctness as part of its results, and ideally report results under both a content and a format criterion, because instruction following and task competence are different properties: only 28\% of the sampled replies met both.

\textbf{Differential testing finds almost everything and decides almost nothing.} With three weak models from different families, nearly every failure produced a disagreement, so the differential oracle had near-perfect recall. This is the setting in which differential testing is most effective at detection and least informative about blame: in half of the disagreements no model was right, so neither a majority vote nor trust in the strongest model would have produced the correct answer. Csmith made disagreements decisive by restricting generation to programs with defined behavior \citep{yang2011csmith}; our analogue is restricting generation to tasks with a computable answer. The two strategies share the same trade-off: decisiveness is bought by narrowing the input space. Consensus-wrong prompts, the blind spot that motivated combining models with ground truth \citep{zhang2023sac3}, were rare in our data (one prompt), probably because the models fail in different ways. With stronger and more similar models we would expect more shared errors and lower differential recall.

\textbf{Paraphrases expose a failure class that the other oracles do not name.} The metamorphic oracle was the more precise of the two ground-truth-free oracles, and its signal has a direct interpretation: the model's answer depends on wording that should not matter. A change of the closing instruction alone changed Qwen's answer (Table~\ref{tab:example}), which mirrors the formatting sensitivity reported for larger models \citep{sclar2024formatting}. For a developer, a metamorphic inconsistency is also a reproducible bug report: two prompts, same task, different answers. Its blind spot is the consistently wrong model, which is the case where ground truth or a second model is needed.

\textbf{Model size did not predict robustness.} TinyLlama, the largest model tested (1.1\,B parameters), had the lowest pass rate, mostly because it ignored output instructions: it explained, wrote code, or wrapped JSON in prose. SmolLM2, the smallest (360\,M), copied hard Unicode payloads better than Qwen, which rewrote or translated them. Differences in instruction tuning appear to matter more than size at this scale. These comparisons hold for our prompts and decoding configuration only, and we do not claim a ranking of the models in general.

\textbf{Practical implications.} For practitioners who embed small chat models, the results suggest three checks that a grammar-based campaign can automate cheaply: whether the model follows output-format instructions, whether its answer is stable under paraphrase, and whether it preserves non-ASCII text it is asked to copy. For researchers, the results suggest that the effectiveness of LLM fuzzing should be evaluated with known-answer tasks before trusting differential or metamorphic signals on open-ended ones, because recall and precision of these oracles can be measured only there.

\textbf{Limits of the technique.} The approach covers only tasks whose answer can be computed from the generated values. The \code{edge} category shows what lies outside that scope: hallucinated translations, unwarranted refusals, degenerate repetition, and template leakage occurred in 57 of 90 replies, and none was detected automatically. Grammar coverage, finally, measures the variety of request templates, not the variety of model behaviors; RQ3 shows that guidance improves the former, while its effect on the latter remains small at our scale.
