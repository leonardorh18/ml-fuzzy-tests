Part 1: How does CheckList relate to prior work? Each previous approach covers one piece of the problem. Held-out accuracy on benchmarks such as SST-2 or QQP is the standard, but test sets share the biases of the training data and a single number hides where models fail. Robustness evaluations, with typos, noise or adversarial examples, focus on one capability. Evaluations of fairness or logical consistency, such as swapping names, are usually built for one task at a time. Challenge sets, mostly for natural language inference, give systematic control, but they are small, often unlike real data, and target hard cases; CheckList keeps the control through templates, adds real unlabeled data through INV and DIR, and focuses on cases that should be easy. Probes train classifiers on internal representations: they show what a model encodes, not how it behaves on the end task. For example, an NER probe on BERT reaches 96.7% (Tenney et al., 2019), yet BERT fine-tuned on QQP or SST-2 fails basic named-entity tests. Finally, the inspiration comes from software engineering: behavioral testing (Beizer, 1995) and metamorphic testing (Segura et al., 2016); MFTs correspond to unit tests, and INV and DIR to metamorphic tests. The authors are also explicit about what CheckList does not cover: labeling errors, data versioning, worst-case security and interpretability. Next, Part 2 explains the key concepts.
