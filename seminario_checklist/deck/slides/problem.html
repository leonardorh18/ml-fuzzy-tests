BERT: 92.7% on SST-2 — yet fails 85% of “I thought the plane would be awful, but it wasn’t.”

Ribeiro et al. (2020), §1 and Table 1

Part 1: Let me start with the problem that motivated the paper. NLP models are usually evaluated by their accuracy on a held-out test set, often on public leaderboards. The authors point out three weaknesses. First, test sets share the biases of the training data, so real-world performance can be much lower than reported. Second, a single number does not tell us where the model fails or how to fix it. Third, the alternative evaluations that existed, such as robustness or fairness tests, each focus on one task or one capability. Here is a concrete example from the paper: BERT reaches 92.7% accuracy on the SST-2 sentiment benchmark, yet it fails 85% of simple sentences where the negation comes at the end, like “I thought the plane would be awful, but it wasn’t”. The authors’ answer is to test NLP models the way we test software.
